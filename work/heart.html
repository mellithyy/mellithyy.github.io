Heart Risk Model

15 years of US surveys, tested on a year it never saw

The problem

Can 15 years of US health surveys estimate a person’s heart-disease risk honestly?

What I built

One dataset from 15 yearly CDC files (6.7 million answers), a model trained on past years and a public what-if app.

The result

ROC‑AUC 0.851 on 2025, a year the model never saw.

What it is

A heart disease risk estimate from 22 everyday answers, built on 15 years of the US CDC’s health survey and tested the way it would be used: once, on a year it had never seen.

The question

Every year the CDC asks between 356,000 and 506,000 US adults about their health. Fifteen years of those answers can show how heart disease and its risk factors move, and how far everyday answers point to it. But only once the yearly files become one dataset: questions are renamed, recoded and asked in some years only.

The app shows a person’s estimate next to people of the same age and gender, and what moves it. It is for anyone curious about their own answers, and it says plainly that it is not a diagnosis.

What it does

15 files merged

The CDC publishes one file per year, 1.3 GB zipped in all, and renames and recodes questions over time. A harmonisation map gives, for each standard variable, the raw name to use in each year and how to translate its codes. The result: 6,705,004 adults by 31 columns.

Heart disease looks flat, but it is falling

As reported, 6.6% of adults in 2011 and 6.8% in 2025. The population got older over those years; once age is taken out, the rate fell from 7.1% to 6.3%. The zig-zag is the survey, not health: the CDC alternates two questionnaires between odd and even years.

Most risk factors got worse

Obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure and depression all rose between 2011 and 2025. Two moved the other way: adults who ever smoked fell from 44.7% to 35.1%, and those who do no exercise from 25.6% to 22.4%.

The strongest links hold for 15 years

How much more often people with each condition report heart disease, against people of the same age group and gender (odds ratios). The order stays the same throughout and most links eased a little. High cholesterol eased the most in relative terms, 2.7 to 2.2 (−17%), perhaps because more people are treated; the survey cannot prove why.

The score holds on years it never saw

Blood pressure and cholesterol are asked in odd years only, so the even years were never used for training. Scored on all seven of them, 3.1 million people, a model without those two questions keeps ROC‑AUC between 0.842 and 0.853.

Its probabilities match reality closely

When the model says about 20%, about 18 to 20% of those people reported heart disease. Each dot is a group of the 352,145 people in 2025 with similar estimates; a dot on the line means the estimate matched reality.

What moves an estimate

One example through the saved model, the same call the app makes: a man aged 55 to 59 in good general health, BMI 27, with high blood pressure, who smokes and does not exercise; nothing else. His estimate is 5.9%. Each bar changes one answer. Exercise points the “wrong” way: people already diagnosed are often told to exercise, so these are associations, not advice.

Application

22 answers on one side, the estimate against people of the same age and gender, and what moves it. Nothing entered is saved.

How it works

  1. 01Download 15 yearly files from cdc.gov, 1.3 GB zipped.
  2. 02Harmonise One map turns every year’s names and codes into the same 31 columns.
  3. 03Check A data-quality report per variable and year; the target matches the CDC’s own variable.
  4. 04Train On the odd years 2011 to 2021, 2.72 million people; tuned on 2023.
  5. 05Test once Refit on 2011 to 2023, then one test on 2025: 352,145 people never seen.
  6. 06The app Streamlit, the saved model and small result files; no 6.7-million-row dataset needed.
  • 22 answers go in: age, gender, race, BMI, general health, physical-health days, mental-health days, walking, smoking, drinking, exercise, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, stroke, kidney disease, COPD, asthma, arthritis, depression, income, education.
  • Medication and the last check-up are left out on purpose: people take medicine and see doctors because they already have heart disease, so those answers leak the diagnosis.
  • No resampling: the cut-off is chosen on 2023, which keeps the probabilities true.

How I checked it

A model is only as honest as its test, and the first test was of my own earlier work. My 2022 version of this project reported F1 0.91 and 95% accuracy. Rerunning it showed where those numbers came from: the data was undersampled before it was split, which removed the hardest “No” cases from the test set too and raised its share of heart disease from 9% to 25%. On a fair test the same model scores F1 0.29. This version splits by year before anything else and never resamples.

  • The heart disease answer matches the CDC’s own variable for every one of 352,145 people in 2025.
  • Scored on the seven even years it never trained on, the model without blood pressure and cholesterol keeps ROC‑AUC between 0.842 and 0.853.
  • Spot check: for 634 men aged 60 to 64 with high blood pressure, smoking and no exercise, the app says 9.3% and their real rate is 9.3%.
  • Weighted to the US adult population, ROC‑AUC is 0.872.
  • The tests run on GitHub on every push.

Limits

  • The answers are self-reported, and some (stroke, poor health days) may follow heart disease rather than cause it. The model shows associations, not causes, and it is about US adults; rates differ elsewhere, the Gulf included. It is not a diagnostic tool.

What I would do differently

One shared cut-off finds far fewer cases among women and younger adults: recall 0.40 for women against 0.62 for men. A real screening tool would set its cut-offs per group, with clinicians.
