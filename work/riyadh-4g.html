Riyadh 4G Benchmark

Three networks compared fairly on 2.7 million phone readings

The problem

Which of Riyadh’s three 4G networks really serves people best, where they use their phones?

What I built

An audit of 2.7 million phone readings, a fair cell‑by‑cell comparison and a one‑file dashboard.

The result

27.5% of the readings removed by the audit before any comparison; the fair test favours better signal, not more traffic.

What it is

A benchmark of Riyadh’s three 4G networks built on readings that phones sent in, with the audit that had to come first and a dashboard anyone can open offline.

The question

Better coverage should carry more traffic: weak signal, worse experience, less data used. If that holds, it tells a network where to invest.

The first look agreed: signal strength and downlink traffic moved together. But busy districts both use more data and receive more network investment, so the question needed a fair test before anyone spent money on it.

What it does

Data audited first

Nine rules run in order, and each one logs the rows it removes and why. 2,725,353 raw readings become 1,975,572 that can be analysed: 27.5% go, and every step can be seen.

Finds what is not coverage

One handset model on Operator A, the Galaxy Note8, reports only a few fixed signal values. They show up as three spikes that look like a coverage pattern and are not one. The model is flagged, and a switch in the dashboard takes it out: Operator A’s share of poor readings falls from 17.1% to 8.4%.

Removes 14 rows that would flip the ranking

Fourteen traffic records each claim about 104,000 MB inside fifteen minutes, close to 925 Mbps held on one phone. Left in, they make Operator B carry the most traffic, 4.1 times its real total. Taken out, Operator A carries the most.

Compares like with like

The city is cut into 1 km cells, and the networks are compared only where each of the three has at least 150 readings: 306 cells. No network is judged on a place where it was simply measured less. In those cells Operator C has the strongest signal in 173.

Tests the question properly

Traffic against signal inside the same 1 km cell and the same hour (fixed effects), so a business district is never weighed against a quiet suburb, and a stricter model that pairs every cell with every hour. 10 dB more signal comes with 2.4% less downlink traffic, p = 0.57: no real effect. The one estimate under p = 0.05 points the other way.

Forecasts the next 24 hours

For each operator and direction: 72 observed hours, then the next 24 from a SARIMA model. The shaded range is the 80% interval, about 1.8 times the central estimate. It is wide on purpose: three days of data cannot honestly support a tighter one.

Checks the forecast against five others

Five rivals, from repeating the last hour to Prophet and gradient boosting, scored on the 24 hours ahead. SARIMA has the lowest error, MASE 0.76, about 24% better than repeating the same hour of the day before.

The dashboard

Four pages and twenty linked views in one file. Picking an operator re-frames every view, the date range sets the weekend against weekdays, both data switches recompute the charts they touch, and every chart has a table behind it.

How it works

  1. 01Phone readings 2,725,353 signal readings and 138,469 traffic records over three days
  2. 02Audit Nine rules in order; each one logs its rows and its reason
  3. 031 km grid Readings binned into cells; a fair cell needs 150 readings from every operator
  4. 04Benchmark Each network against the best rival in the same cell, and a ranked action list
  5. 05Test and forecast Fixed-effects models for signal and traffic; SARIMA against five other forecasts
  6. 06One-file dashboard Four pages and twenty linked views in one HTML file that runs offline
Two defects that can be corrected stay as switches, so their effect is seen, not assumed

How I checked it

Every exclusion and every comparison had to survive a stricter version of itself.

  • Every removed row is logged with its rule, its count and its reason.
  • The two fixable defects are switches in the dashboard, so their effect can be seen.
  • Networks are compared only where each has at least 150 readings in the cell.
  • The null result holds for downlink and uplink, and in the stricter cell-by-hour model.
  • The forecast is scored against five other models, 1 hour and 24 hours ahead.
  • The forecast range is reported wide on purpose.

Limits

  • No user IDs: readings are not people. One heavy phone can send thousands, and ten handset models give 57.4% of the readings.
  • Three days is the binding limit: no weekly pattern, no holidays, no trend.
  • Crowdsourced readings follow where people are, not where the network is; that is why the comparison waits for 150 readings per network.

What I would do differently

I would collect a few weeks before forecasting, and add per-cell throughput and connected-user counts, which sit closer to what users feel.
